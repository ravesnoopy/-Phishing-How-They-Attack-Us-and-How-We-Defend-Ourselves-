# What Is Phishing?

## The core definition

Phishing is a form of **social engineering** — a psychological manipulation technique, not a technical hacking technique — in which an attacker impersonates a trusted entity (a bank, a coworker, the IT department, a government agency, a well-known brand) in order to trick a victim into taking an action that benefits the attacker.

That action is almost always one of the following:

- Handing over credentials (username and password)
- Clicking a malicious link that leads to a fake login page or drops malware
- Opening an infected attachment
- Approving or executing a fraudulent payment or wire transfer
- Revealing sensitive personal, financial, or corporate information
- Installing a malicious app, browser extension, or remote access tool

## Why the name "phishing"

The term is a deliberate play on "fishing." The attacker casts a wide net (or a very precise, targeted line) baited with something that looks legitimate and appealing — a trustworthy sender, a convincing logo, a familiar tone of voice — and waits for someone to bite. The "ph" spelling traces back to 1990s hacker culture ("phreaking," the manipulation of phone systems), and the metaphor has held up remarkably well for three decades because it captures the essential mechanic of the attack: **bait, hook, catch.**

## Phishing is not a technology problem — it's a trust problem

This is the single most important idea to internalize, and it's the foundation for everything else in this repository. Firewalls, antivirus software, and spam filters are designed to stop malicious *code*. Phishing doesn't primarily rely on malicious code to succeed — it relies on malicious *persuasion*. The attacker's real target is not a server or a database; it's the decision-making process of a human being in the three to five seconds it takes to glance at an email and decide whether to click.

This reframes the entire problem. You cannot "patch" a human being the way you patch software. You can only train, prepare, and build processes that reduce the odds that a moment of distraction turns into a breach.

## A simple mental model

Think of phishing as a con game that has been digitized and scaled:

1. **The disguise** — the attacker looks exactly like someone you'd normally trust.
2. **The script** — the message follows a predictable emotional pattern (urgency, fear, authority, curiosity, reward).
3. **The ask** — a specific, low-friction action: click, log in, confirm, download, approve.
4. **The payoff** — credentials, money, data, or a foothold inside your network.

Every phishing email, text message, phone call, or fake website you will see in this repository follows that same four-step structure, no matter how sophisticated it looks on the surface.

## Why this matters before we go further

Before diving into statistics, attack types, and real-world breaches, it's worth sitting with this definition for a moment: **phishing succeeds because it exploits trust, not because it defeats technology.** Every recommendation in this repository — from MFA to reporting culture to department-specific training — exists to rebuild the friction that phishing is specifically designed to remove.
